Section A — Hydraulic System Description

Build the system from first principles: fluid is stored, conditioned, pressurized, routed and converted into mechanical work.

A1System purpose, layout and centre typesWhat hydraulics do, basic flow paths, open-centre vs closed-centre⌄
Core principle: the pump supplies fluid flow. Pressure develops when that flow meets resistance.
Why aircraft use hydraulicsLarge force from compact equipment, flexible routing through lines, accurate control and the ability to power several services.
Typical usersFlight controls, landing gear, brakes, steering, flaps, spoilers and other mechanisms requiring controlled force.
Energy conversionMechanical input → hydraulic energy → mechanical output at an actuator.

Basic line functions

Supply / suctionReservoir to pump inlet.
PressurePump or pressure source to system users.
ReturnUsed fluid back to the reservoir.
Case drainReturns internal leakage or cooling flow from some components to the reservoir.

Open-centre vs closed-centre

FeatureOpen-centreClosed-centre
Neutral conditionFluid has an open return path.Valve blocks the flow path; pressure is available for demand.
Pressure at restNormally low while flow circulates.Maintained or controlled by the pump/regulating arrangement.
Typical logicWork begins when the open path is restricted and flow is directed to a unit.Opening a selected path sends pressure to the unit.
Primary source: Section A, pp. 11.1–11.3
A2Hydraulic fluids: properties, types and compatibilityViscosity, stability, fire characteristics and correct-fluid discipline⌄

What hydraulic fluid must do

  • Transmit power.
  • Lubricate moving parts.
  • Help seal clearances.
  • Carry away heat.
  • Remain stable through the approved operating range.
ViscosityResistance to flow. Too high increases resistance; too low can reduce lubrication and sealing effectiveness.
Chemical stabilityResistance to unwanted chemical change during service.
Flash pointTemperature at which vapour can ignite momentarily.
Fire pointTemperature at which burning continues after ignition.

Main fluid families in the source

FamilySource examples / identificationRevision point
Mineral-basedMIL-H-5606; commonly redPetroleum-based and comparatively flammable.
Polyalphaolefin-basedMIL-H-83282Developed with improved fire-resistance characteristics.
Phosphate-ester-basedCommercial products such as SkydrolFire-resistant, but requires compatible seals, hoses, paints and servicing equipment.
Do not mix or substitute fluid types. Compatibility with system materials, seals, hoses, paints and servicing equipment is essential. Colour alone must not be treated as a reliable identification method.

Contamination effect

Particles, water, air or incompatible chemicals can increase wear, obstruct small passages, damage seals, alter fluid properties and produce unreliable operation.

Primary source: Section A, pp. 11.3–11.7
A3Reservoirs and accumulatorsFluid storage, pressurization and stored hydraulic energy⌄

Reservoir functions

  • Store and supply hydraulic fluid.
  • Receive return fluid.
  • Allow for thermal expansion and system-volume changes.
  • Support deaeration, cooling and contaminant settling where the design permits.
Reservoir typeKey characteristicWhy it matters
Non-pressurizedVented to atmosphere through a controlled arrangement.The pump must still receive adequate inlet flow throughout aircraft operation.
Air-pressurizedAir pressure acts on the fluid surface.Provides positive pump inlet pressure, especially at altitude.
Fluid-pressurizedA small piston/area relationship uses system fluid pressure to pressurize reservoir fluid.Maintains positive pressure without a separate air source.

Accumulator

An accumulator stores hydraulic energy by compressing a gas charge separated from hydraulic fluid by a diaphragm, bladder or piston.

System pressure enters→Gas compresses→Energy stored→Gas expands when pressure falls
Store energyProvide a limited reserve of pressurized fluid.
Dampen pulsationsSmooth pressure fluctuations and pump pulsations.
Compensate leakageSupply small losses and reduce frequent cycling.
Emergency supportOperate selected services for a limited period, depending on the aircraft.
Stored-energy hazard: an accumulator may remain pressurized after the pump is switched off. Depressurize and service only by the approved procedure.
Primary source: Section A, pp. 11.7–11.12
A4Filters, indicators and heat exchangersKeep fluid clean, detect restriction and control heat⌄

Filtration

Filters remove particles before they damage close-clearance components. They may be installed in pressure, return or case-drain lines.

Filter elementTraps particles while allowing fluid to pass.
Differential-pressure indicatorResponds to the pressure difference across a restricted element.
Bypass valveAllows flow around a severely restricted element so the system is not starved.
Thermal lockoutPrevents a false clogged-filter indication caused by cold, high-viscosity fluid.
Cause-and-effect: contamination increases filter restriction → differential pressure rises → indicator activates or the bypass opens, depending on the design.

Heat exchangers

A heat exchanger transfers hydraulic-fluid heat to another medium. The source shows installations associated with aircraft fuel systems.

Primary source: Section A, pp. 11.12–11.14
A5Power distribution and control valvesDirection, sequence, source selection and protection⌄
Selector valveRoutes pressure and return to control an actuator's direction.
Check valvePermits flow in one direction and prevents reverse flow.
Orifice check valveFree flow one way; restricted flow the other way.
Sequence valveEnsures one operation occurs before another.
Priority valvePreserves pressure for essential services when supply is limited.
Shuttle valveConnects one of two pressure sources to a common outlet.
Quick-disconnectAllows separation with minimum loss and reduced contamination entry.
Hydraulic fuseStops or limits excessive downstream fluid loss after abnormal high flow.

Four-way closed-centre selector valve

Its four main connections are pressure, return and two actuator ports. In one selected position, one actuator side receives pressure and the other returns fluid; the connections reverse for the opposite movement.

Do not confuseCorrect distinction
Check valve vs shuttle valveCheck valve controls direction; shuttle valve chooses between two sources.
Priority valve vs sequence valvePriority protects essential pressure; sequence controls order of operation.
Hydraulic fuse vs relief valveFuse responds to excessive flow/loss; relief valve responds to excessive pressure.
Primary source: Section A, pp. 11.14–11.19
A6Hydraulic actuatorsConvert fluid pressure into linear or rotary movement⌄
Actuator function: convert hydraulic energy into mechanical force or motion.
TypeHow it worksReturn / reverse method
Single-acting cylinderPressure acts on one side of the piston.Spring, gravity or external load returns it.
Double-acting cylinderPressure can be applied to either piston side.Selector valve reverses pressure and return.
Rotary actuatorProduces angular movement rather than a long straight stroke.Hydraulic flow is reversed or redirected depending on design.

Double-acting flow logic

Pressure to left chamber→Piston extends+Right chamber returns
Pressure to right chamber→Piston retracts+Left chamber returns
Primary source: Section A, pp. 11.19–11.20

B1Pressure generation and pump drivesMechanical and electrical sources, flow versus pressure⌄
Exam distinction: a pump is the source of fluid flow. Restriction to that flow produces hydraulic pressure.
Engine-driven pumpNormally mounted to an engine accessory drive and used as a primary pressure source.
Electric-motor-driven pumpCan support ground operation, backup or other aircraft-specific demands.
Hand pumpManual source for servicing, testing or limited backup functions.
Shear shaft / couplingProtects the drive if the pump seizes by providing a designed failure point.

Positive-displacement concept

Aircraft hydraulic pumps are commonly positive-displacement designs: each cycle moves a defined volume of fluid. Output may be constant or varied by pump geometry.

Constant displacementVariable displacement
Moves a fixed amount per cycle; a regulator/unloading arrangement manages system pressure.Changes displacement to match system demand and can destroke when pressure is satisfied.
Primary source: Section B, pp. 11.20–11.22
B2Pump types and variable-displacement controlGear, gerotor, piston, vane and compensator action⌄
Gear pumpMeshing gears carry fluid around the casing from inlet to outlet.
Gerotor pumpChanging spaces between an inner and outer rotor draw in and discharge fluid.
Axial piston pumpPistons arranged parallel to the shaft reciprocate against an angled plate or block.
Bent-axis pumpAn angular relationship between shaft and cylinder block creates piston stroke.
Inline piston pumpPiston stroke is produced while the piston group remains aligned with the drive axis.
Vane pumpSliding vanes create changing chamber volume as the rotor turns.

Variable-displacement pump logic

Demand / low pressure→Greater yoke or swashplate angle→Longer piston stroke→More output
System pressure reaches setting→Compensator acts→Pump destrokes→Minimum flow
Depressurized mode: an electrically controlled arrangement may hold the compensator in a condition that keeps the pump at minimum output when pressure is not required.
Primary source: Section B, pp. 11.22–11.27
B3Pressure control: relief, regulation and reductionProtection, normal pressure management and lower-pressure branches⌄
DeviceMain functionTypical response
System relief valveProtects against excessive system pressure.Opens at its setting and returns excess fluid.
Thermal relief valveRelieves pressure caused by trapped-fluid thermal expansion.Small quantity bypasses when trapped-line pressure rises.
Pressure regulatorMaintains operating pressure with a constant-delivery pump.Unloads or bypasses pump flow when the pressure range is satisfied.
Pressure-reducing valveSupplies a branch at lower pressure than the main system.Maintains the selected downstream pressure within design limits.
Relief vs regulator: the relief valve is primarily protection. The regulator is part of normal pressure management for a constant-delivery system.
Primary source: Section B, pp. 11.26–11.29
B4Seals, packings, O-rings and installationLeak prevention, material compatibility and damage avoidance⌄
V-ring packingPressure-energized sealing used in assemblies; orientation matters.
U-ring packingUsed in brake and master-cylinder type applications; lip orientation matters.
O-ringCommon circular-section seal for static or moving joints, using the correct material and size.
Backup ringSupports an O-ring and limits extrusion under pressure.
GasketStatic seal between mating surfaces.
WiperExcludes dirt and moisture from exposed moving rods or shafts.

O-ring installation discipline

  1. Verify the correct part, material and size using approved data.
  2. Inspect the O-ring and groove; reject cuts, flattening, deformation or contamination.
  3. Use the specified lubricant or clean system fluid where instructed.
  4. Protect the seal from threads, sharp edges and corners using the correct sleeve or installation tool.
  5. Avoid twisting, excessive stretching and use of sharp tools.
  6. Confirm correct seating and backup-ring orientation where fitted.
Identification caution: O-ring colour can vary by manufacturer and material. Do not identify a seal by colour alone.
Primary source: Section B, pp. 11.28–11.32
B5Indication and warningPressure, quantity, temperature, pump status and maintenance displays⌄
PressureShows whether system or pump pressure is available and within the expected condition.
QuantityReservoir level can be sensed mechanically or electrically; low quantity may indicate leakage or servicing error.
TemperatureOverheat sensing warns of fluid or motor temperature above a preset condition.
Filter conditionDifferential-pressure indication identifies increasing restriction.

Older aircraft may use individual gauges and annunciator lights. Modern aircraft can show system status, synoptic diagrams and maintenance information on electronic displays.

Troubleshooting principle: an indication is evidence of a condition. Confirm the sensor, wiring and system condition before declaring a component faulty.
Primary source: Section B, pp. 11.31–11.33
B6Servicing, contamination and troubleshootingPrevent contamination, interpret symptoms and verify the repair⌄

Four contamination categories in the source

ParticlesCause abrasive wear, block close-clearance parts and plug orifices.
WaterCan react with fluid, promote corrosion and affect lubrication.
AirCan compress, generate heat, reduce efficiency and increase noise.
ChemicalIncorrect fluids or residues can react with hoses, seals, plated parts and the fluid itself.

Contamination prevention

  • Use only the correct clean fluid and dedicated compatible servicing equipment.
  • Keep filler caps, breathers, filters and screens clean and serviceable.
  • Clean connection areas before opening the system; cap or plug lines immediately.
  • Keep containers closed and do not return drained fluid to the system.
  • Follow fluid sampling and monitoring intervals in approved data.

Troubleshooting evidence

Evidence / symptomAreas to investigate
Low pressureFluid supply, pump output, regulator/relief condition, leakage, valve position and indication system.
Low reservoir quantityExternal leakage, servicing condition and component position required by the servicing procedure.
Slow or weak actuatorRestricted flow, filter condition, pump output, internal leakage, valve routing and actuator condition.
High temperatureLow fluid level, cooling, restriction, relief/regulator operation and excessive system operation.
Foam, gurgling or reservoir whirlpoolAeration from low level, air leaks, cold fluid or damaged seals.
High-pitched pump whineCavitation or inadequate inlet conditions; inspect fluid supply and suction path.
Visible fluidTrace the highest/first wet point; fluid may travel away from the true leak source.

Maintenance checklist logic

Fluid condition & level→Filters→Rods & seals→Lines, hoses & fittings→Temperature→Reservoir & pump sound→Sample / test
Finish the job: correct the cause, restore fluid cleanliness, perform the required operational/leak test and verify all indications—not only the original symptom.

Quick Review

Use this immediately before practice questions or an exam.

CLO1 · System description

  • Pump supplies flow; resistance produces pressure.
  • Reservoir stores fluid; accumulator stores pressurized energy.
  • Open-centre circulates in neutral; closed-centre keeps pressure available.
  • Filter restriction creates differential pressure.
  • Selector valve directs movement; check valve prevents reverse flow.
  • Actuator converts hydraulic energy to mechanical movement.

CLO2 · Operation & maintenance

  • Constant-displacement output is controlled by a regulator/unloading system.
  • Variable-displacement output changes with swashplate/yoke angle and compensator action.
  • Relief protects; regulator controls normal pressure; reducer supplies a lower-pressure branch.
  • Correct seal material, size and installation are essential.
  • Contamination categories: particles, water, air and chemical.
  • Confirm → inspect → isolate → test → correct → verify.

High-value distinctions

PairDifference to remember
Reservoir / accumulatorReservoir stores fluid; accumulator stores hydraulic energy using compressed gas.
Check / shuttle valveOne-way flow versus selection between two sources.
Sequence / priority valveOrder of operations versus protection of essential supply.
Relief / regulatorOverpressure protection versus normal pressure management.
External / internal leakageVisible escape versus bypass within a component that reduces performance.
Aeration / cavitationEntrained air and foaming versus vapour/gas bubble collapse from poor inlet or pressure conditions.
